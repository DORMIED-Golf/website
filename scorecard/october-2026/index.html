Good Good Fell 80% and Still Finished Third

Golf lost almost a third of its attention in September. It forgot the spikes and kept two things: a label it watched on 24 players for four days, and a brand it spent August arguing about.

By Adam R. & Travis R. · October 14, 2026

The Lede: The Winner and the Loser Wore the Same Label

When Jacob Bridgeman holed a four-foot par putt on the 18th at Medinah to clinch the Presidents Cup, the man he beat, Min Woo Lee, was dressed by the same company. B. Draddy outfitted both teams. Two weeks earlier it had dressed the American side at the Solheim Cup in the Netherlands, which lost to Europe 15-13. September belonged to team golf. The U.S. erased a three-point deficit on Sunday with a record 9½ singles points for its 11th straight Presidents Cup, and earlier in the month LIV Golf filed for Chapter 11. None of it rescued the index. Attention across all 215 brands fell 30.8%, the steepest drop of the year. Only 20 brands gained ground, the fewest of any month this year, and the total landed below January and February. September is when our game's attention drains out. What it keeps is the story, and this month it kept two very different kinds of brand.

At The Top: Titleist Takes the Crown Back. Good Good Won't Leave the Room.

Titleist is back in the top spot, where it has sat in eight of nine months this year. Its search volume fell 18.3%, which in this market counts as a good month: Titleist's share of all golf brand attention rose from 6.7% to 7.9%. TaylorMade climbed back to second at 67.2 despite a 32.8% fall. Then comes the number we promised to watch. Good Good Golf fell 80% and still finished third. Last month's Long Game laid out two outcomes. Settle below July, and notoriety becomes a liability that reprices every creator deal in golf. Settle above it, and the conclusion gets uncomfortable. Good Good settled at nearly double its July search volume and nine places higher, in the same month its CEO and president departed. One month in, with October still to come, that is the uncomfortable answer. The detail that makes it sting is Callaway. The company that approved the ad and then ended the partnership fell 55.3% and landed exactly on its July search figure. The two finished tied at 54.7 alongside TravisMathew. The attention stayed with the brand that made the ad and left the one that approved it. Further down, Holderness & Bourne retook 10th, matching its best rank ever, and Vice Golf slipped to 12th.

The Biggest Move: B. Draddy Dressed Three Teams and Won the Month

B. Draddy rose 49.6% to 39th, the best rank in its history on our index, in a month when 155 brands went the other way. In the U.S. it jumped from 52nd to 28th, and it climbed in nine of our ten markets. When our news desk examined why the Presidents Cup keeps picking B. Draddy in August, the answer was 200 embroidery heads in Wisconsin, and the brand sat in the 60s with a flat reading. The uniforms had been revealed. The broadcast hadn't happened. It rose 22% in August, the month the Presidents Cup kit was unveiled, and 50% in September, when it was worn. The Solheim Cup doubled as the tournament debut of its new women's line, which is a hard room to launch in. Its parent, Summit Golf Brands, also owns Fairway & Greene and EP NY, the women's label that opened our very first Drop Zone back in March.

Now compare the team that actually won the Solheim Cup. PING, whose founders gave the event its name, has dressed Europe's side at every playing since 2021 and did it again at Bernardus. Its number fell 18.3%, in step with the rest of the top 20. For a club company, a uniform is a rounding error. For an apparel brand, it is the whole month. The last Ryder Cup handed its uniforms to Ralph Lauren and Loro Piana, fashion houses that don't need golf. The Presidents Cup and the American Solheim side chose a golf brand that does, and September showed why that matters to the brand. A major puts one bag on camera for one Sunday. A team event puts the same label on 24 chests for four days and sells the replica the same week.

The Field: A Quiet Climber and a Blade With a Ship Date

Sentinel Golf rose 52.6% to 121st, its best rank ever, and its rank has improved every month since June. The accessories label builds things like shag bags with Madewell Products, the company behind the original Bag Shag. No single story explains the spike.

McLaren Golf climbed 22.2% and 12 places back to 59th, its first gain since April, in the month its Series 1 blade finally got a ship date, five months after launch. That is exactly where it sat in June, so this isn't a recovery yet. It's a pulse, and it arrived with product news rather than a launch party.

The Drop Zone: The Summer's Spikes Came Due

Last month Barstool Golf and Random Golf Club each rose 50% while Good Good Golf collapsed, and we noted that nobody blamed the creator category. In September they gave it all back and then some, falling 56.1% and 45.5%. The halo left with the headlines. Our last three Biggest Move picks before this one fell too: ProSENDR by 45.8%, Carnival Golf by 70% and Good Good by 80%. Carnival's record July surge is mostly unwound, with the brand back at 185th against 199th in June. Honors and Uneekor both reversed hard after August spikes, down 64.1% and 33.1%. Spikes are loans. September is when they get called.

The Long Game: Srixon Gets the Launch Season TaylorMade Is Skipping

Srixon rose 22.2% to 11th, its best rank ever, in a month the index fell 30.8%. We called the Open bump for August and then reported it never came. It came in September, and it came with the clubs. The second-generation ZXi irons were announced on September 8 and the ZXi RKT woods two weeks later. Shane Lowry won the Irish Open by a record 11 shots with the new driver, and a week later J.J. Spaun took the BMW PGA at Wentworth with Srixon irons and ball. The three unreleased clubs Ryan Fox won The Open with reach shelves on October 16. Srixon is now tied with Holderness & Bourne on DI at 30.1 and sits 11th on the tiebreak. Meanwhile TaylorMade has said it won't launch a new driver in 2027, which Golf Digest reckons will be its first year without one since 2001. Our call: Srixon cracks the top ten for the first time before January's launch season is over. A major won with unreleased clubs is a trailer. October 16 is the release.

Global Dispatch: Korea Didn't Get the Memo

South Korea was the only market on the index to grow in September, up 7.6%, or 8.7% with Good Good Golf stripped out. Two brands did almost all of it. PXG, first in Korea, and Malbon, third, added 1,420 of the market's 1,440 net new searches between them. Malbon is the striking one: down 32.7% globally, nearly doubled in Korea. Our first issue flagged PXG leading Korea while scoring 30.1 globally, and in June we wrote that Korea might be Malbon's core business rather than its hedge. September is the strongest evidence yet for both. The traffic runs the other way too. Khalhon, the Seoul label we profiled in September for its Sean Wotherspoon capsule, rose 50%, and more than half of its searches came from the United States. A Los Angeles brand is growing in Seoul while a Seoul brand grows in America. In golf, the home market is no longer a safe guess for where a brand's growth lives.

The Closing Note: Uniforms and Notoriety

When Jacob Bridgeman's putt dropped, half the players on the green at Medinah celebrated and half didn't, and the company on every one of their chests won either way. That is the cleanest version of what September taught. Our game lost almost a third of its attention in thirty days, and the biggest things it let go of were spikes: the halo brands, the record surges, the scandal's bystanders. What it held onto was a label it watched for four days on 24 players, and a brand it spent August arguing about. One kind of attention took two Presidents Cups and 200 embroidery heads in Wisconsin to build. The other arrived in an afternoon and hasn't left. Golf remembers what it wore and what it argued about. The spikes, it forgot by October.

Adam and Travis, DORMIED
