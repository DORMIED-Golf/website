The One Thing Golfers Talk About and Never Measure. The Stack System Just Put a Number on It.

The Stack System launched Practice Tracker free with its $289 Stack Radar, adding a consistency metric called Spread to a category built on subscriptions.

By Travis R.  ·  September 1, 2026  ·  Speed Training  ·  3 min read

Image: The Golf Wire

What Happened

The Stack System launched Practice Tracker, a free feature for all Stack Radar owners on iOS and Android. It logs carry, ball speed, club speed and smash factor by club and intent, and introduces Spread, a consistency metric in yards. Stack Radar stays $289 with no recurring cost, unusual in a category built on annual software fees.

Consumer launch monitors converged on a single business model somewhere around 2021: sell the hardware near cost, charge for the software forever. The device is the entry fee, the app is the annuity, and the second year of ownership is where the margin lives. Almost every player in the sub-$1,000 bracket runs some version of it. The Stack System just launched its most significant software feature in years and attached no price to it at all.

Practice Tracker went live this week for every Stack Radar owner on iOS and Android. It records carry distance, ball speed, club speed and smash factor on every tracked shot, organizes those shots into sessions, and builds a longitudinal record of how a golfer's numbers move over weeks and months. No subscription, no upgrade tier, no data cap. Stack Radar remains $289 with no recurring cost, works indoors or out, and reads dry swings without a ball. Setup, per the company, runs under 60 seconds.

The genuinely new thing in the release is not the tracking. It is Spread, a consistency metric expressed in yards. A golfer averaging 160 with a Spread of plus or minus eight can expect roughly 95 percent of shots to finish between 152 and 168. That framing implies a standard deviation of about four yards, which is a statistically ordinary way to describe dispersion and an unusual way to sell it to golfers. Consistency has been the most-discussed and least-quantified quality in amateur golf for decades. Launch monitors have always been capable of producing this number. What they have not done is track it across a season and show a player whether it is shrinking.

The more interesting engineering decision is the intent tagging. Practice Tracker separates shots by category: Stock, Warm-Up, Max, Fade, Draw. Anyone who has tried to build a gapping chart from raw range data knows why that matters. Warm-up swings and max-effort speed work are the two largest sources of contamination in any practice dataset, and they pull in opposite directions. Arccos ran into the same class of problem on the course side and had to build shot detection and filtering logic before its strokes gained output meant anything. Dirty inputs are how practice logs die. Stack solved it by asking the golfer to declare intent before hitting, which is unglamorous and probably correct.

The business logic behind giving it away is not complicated. The Stack's actual asset is its swing database, now past 50 million recorded swings, which is what makes its speed training prescriptions individualized rather than generic. Every barrier between a golfer and a tracked session is a barrier between the company and more data. Charging for the tracker would have suppressed exactly the usage the platform is built on. There is also a defensive read: Stack sells a training protocol, and the single hardest thing a training product has to prove is that it is working. A tool that shows club speed trending up over eight weeks is a retention mechanism dressed as a feature.

What the company has not solved is being heard. Stack sits at 166th of 215 brands on the DORMIED Index with a flat month, which is roughly where a technically credible product with almost no consumer marketing volume tends to land. Sasho MacKenzie and Marty Jertson have built one of the few training products in golf with published biomechanics behind it, and the brand still operates at a fraction of the share of voice held by companies selling neoprene and vague promises.

The thing to watch is what happens when Practice Tracker starts making recommendations. Right now it measures. The moment it interprets, the moment it tells a golfer which club to work on and what to change, it becomes precisely the product every competitor in this category charges $99 to $199 a year for. Stack has drawn a clear line at no recurring cost. The next feature release will show whether that line was a principle or a positioning move.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Spread in the Stack Practice Tracker?

Spread is a consistency metric expressed in yards. A golfer averaging 160 yards with a Spread of plus or minus eight can expect roughly 95 percent of shots to finish between 152 and 168, which implies a standard deviation of about four yards.

Does Practice Tracker cost extra?

No. It is included free with every Stack Radar, with no subscription, no upgrade tier and no data cap. Stack Radar itself is $289 with no recurring cost.

Why does Practice Tracker separate shots by intent?

Warm-up swings and maximum-effort speed work are the two biggest sources of contamination in practice data and skew results in opposite directions. Categories such as Stock, Warm-Up, Max, Fade and Draw keep stock yardages clean and speed work tracked separately.

Why would The Stack System give the feature away instead of charging for it?

The company's core asset is its swing database, now past 50 million recorded swings, and any paywall would suppress the usage that feeds it. A tracker that shows club speed trending upward also functions as a retention tool for the speed training program.

Where does The Stack System rank on the DORMIED Index?

166th of 215 brands, flat month over month, which is a typical position for a technically credible product with limited consumer marketing volume.
