MacGregor's Toney Penna CB Was One of the Longest Irons in the Test. That's the Problem.

MacGregor's Toney Penna Tour Forged CB finished last in MyGolfSpy's 2026 player's iron test. Distance explains why. The brand's history explains the rest.

By Travis R.  ·  August 3, 2026  ·  Clubs  ·  3 min read

Image: MyGolfSpy

What Happened With MacGregor?

MacGregor's Toney Penna Tour Forged CB finished last in MyGolfSpy's 2026 player's iron test with a 7.9 overall score, the only iron across four 2026 categories to score in the sevens. Distance led at 9.4, but accuracy tied for last at 7.3 and forgiveness came in at 7.7, wrong tradeoffs for a compact forged cavity-back.

A 9.4 distance score next to a 7.3 accuracy score is not bad luck. It is a design brief. Compact forged cavity-backs that outdrive their category are almost always doing it with loft, sole width, or a thinner face than the shape suggests, and the bill for that arrives in dispersion and carry consistency. That is exactly the shape of MacGregor's result in MyGolfSpy's 2026 player's iron test.

How Did MacGregor Score in the MyGolfSpy 2026 Iron Test?

The MacGregor Toney Penna Tour Forged CB finished last in the player's iron category with a 7.9 overall score, the only iron across MyGolfSpy's four 2026 iron categories to land in the sevens. Distance was the high point at 9.4. Accuracy tied for last at 7.3, forgiveness came in at 7.7. It appears alongside the Fourteen TC-826 Forged, the Stix Perform Series 2.0, the PXG 0311 XP GEN8 and the TaylorMade Qi Max HL on a list of irons the testing house says buyers should think twice about. Of that group, MacGregor is the only brand without a functioning tour presence, a national fitting network, or enough share of voice to change the subject.

The naming choice makes the result sting more than the numbers alone would. Toney Penna was MacGregor's tour rep and clubmaker through the era when the company's irons were in the bags of the best players in the world, and putting his name on a compact forged CB is an explicit claim about craftsmanship and precision. That is the product promise. The test returned speed instead. Golfers shopping a small forged head are buying predictable proximity and repeatable gapping, not a full club of extra carry they did not ask for. Distance is the easiest performance metric to engineer into an iron and the least relevant one in this specific category.

Why Do Heritage Golf Brand Revivals Struggle?

Heritage revivals in golf equipment have a consistent failure mode, and it is rarely the name. Ben Hogan Golf's 2015 relaunch under Terry Koehler produced a genuinely well-received forged iron, stamped with actual lofts instead of numbers, and still ended up in bankruptcy court inside two years because the brand had no distribution and no fitting infrastructure to convert interest into orders. Nike's 2016 hardware exit went the other direction: enormous distribution, no reason for a serious iron buyer to pick it. MacGregor's problem is closer to the first case with an added complication. The name has been bought, sold, and licensed across territories repeatedly since the company's 2009 restructuring, which means the equity in the MacGregor script is now doing work in several markets at several price points simultaneously. Nostalgia does not survive that kind of dilution intact.

There is a business mechanic here worth noticing. A negative aggregation post from a testing house with real traffic and affiliate links is a permanent search result, and for a brand this quiet it will be the single highest-visibility mention of the product all year. Brands with tour staffs and media budgets absorb a bad test result because they have twenty other messages in market. MacGregor does not. A global rank of 81st out of 215 in the DORMIED Index with a score under three points, down 18.5 percent month over month, describes a brand that has almost no counterweight to a bad data point. The test result does not just sit alongside the marketing. It becomes the marketing.

Which Category Should MacGregor Target Next?

The strategic question is whether MacGregor keeps fighting for shelf space in the one category where it has the least room for error. A player's iron is a fitting-led, precision-led purchase, and it is the hardest segment in golf to win without a tour validation loop or a retail bay to hit balls in. MacGregor's actual competitive assets are name recognition among golfers over fifty and access to value-channel distribution, which points toward a different product and a different price. Watch what the next iron looks like. If it arrives as another compact forged head chasing ball speed, the 2026 result will repeat, and the Penna name will have been spent on it twice.
