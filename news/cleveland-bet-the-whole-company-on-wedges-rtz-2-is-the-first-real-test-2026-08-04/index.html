Cleveland Bet the Whole Company on Wedges. RTZ 2 Is the First Real Test.

Cleveland Golf's RTZ 2 wedges bring back the 588 grind and expand to five soles. The first real test of the brand's exit from woods and irons.

By Travis R.  ·  August 4, 2026  ·  Clubs  ·  4 min read

Image: MyGolfSpy

What Happened With Cleveland Golf?

Cleveland Golf's RTZ 2 wedge line, arriving at retail September 4 with pre-orders open, is the first launch since Cleveland exited woods and irons eight months ago. It expands sole grinds from three to five, revives the 588 as a grind, introduces Z-Alloy material, and prices Tour Rack Raw at $219.99 versus $199.99 for satin finishes.

Companies that exit two product categories don't get to launch incremental products in the one they kept. Cleveland Golf announced eight months ago it was leaving woods and irons to concentrate on wedges and putters, which means the RTZ 2 line, arriving at retail Sept. 4 with pre-orders open now, is the first product to carry the weight of that decision.

How Does the Cleveland 588 Grind Return in RTZ 2?

The headline feature is the return of the 588, though not as a standalone model. It arrives as a fifth sole grind inside the RTZ 2 architecture: more bounce in the toe, less in the heel, a full sole with moderate bounce and trailing-edge relief, aimed at shallower deliveries. Anyone expecting a reissued 1988 blade will be mildly deflated. Anyone who has actually hit an original 588 will recognize what Cleveland is doing, which is reverse-engineering a sole shape that sold in the millions before Bob Vokey existed as a consumer-facing name, and grafting it onto a modern cast body. Roger Cleveland returned to the company he founded in 1979 a little over a year ago, and the fact that the RTZ 2 soles are being marketed as "Roger Cleveland Signature Grinds" tells you the arrangement is not ceremonial.

The more consequential change is the grind count itself. Cleveland spent years offering three sole options: Low, Mid, Full. That worked when the wedge category was less specialized. It stopped working the moment Vokey pushed to six grinds and every serious fitter started building wedge sessions around sole selection instead of loft gapping. Going from three to five closes most of that gap. It also gives green grass shops and independent fitters a reason to stock Cleveland wedges beyond price, which they have historically done as the value option under Titleist. Five grinds plus three finishes plus lofts from 46 to 64 degrees is a real fitting matrix, and it is the first time Cleveland has asked to be judged on fitting depth rather than on shelf value.

Why Did Cleveland Golf Develop the Z-Alloy Wedge?

The engineering claim worth interrogating is Z-Alloy, developed under metallurgist Boeing Smith as an alternative to the 8620 carbon steel that has been the default wedge material for roughly seven decades. Cleveland says it's lower density, about 10 percent softer in feel, and reduces groove wear by 87 percent. Softer and more durable is the combination brands routinely promise and rarely deliver, and it's the exact claim that independent durability testing will either validate or dismantle within a season. There's also an irony buried in it: Z-Alloy doesn't rust, which means the Tour Rack Raw wedges won't patina. A meaningful slice of the raw wedge market buys raw precisely to watch the head go orange. Cleveland has engineered away the thing some of its customers were paying for.

Speaking of paying, the Tour Rack Raw sits at $219.99 against $199.99 for Black Satin and Tour Satin, and the explanation is the most useful manufacturing detail in the entire launch. Plated heads are molded slightly undersize, come out of milling technically non-conforming, and require plating to reach USGA spec. Raw heads need their own tooling to finish at final dimension. Two molds, lower volume on one of them, higher unit cost. That is not a premium tax dressed as craft. It's a genuine explanation of why the unfinished club costs more, and it's the kind of thing most OEMs never bother to explain because they assume nobody's asking.

Which Categories Still Reward Cleveland Golf's Heritage?

Cleveland's visibility slipped 18 percent month over month in June across DORMIED's tracking, which is what a September launch looks like from the wrong side of the calendar. The interesting question isn't the pre-order window. It's whether a brand that sold drivers as recently as the HiBore era can rebuild an identity as a two-category specialist, and whether the trade treats a narrowed Cleveland as focused or diminished. Wedges and putters are the two categories where brand heritage still moves units. Cleveland has more of that heritage than almost anyone and spent fifteen years pointing it at drivers. RTZ 2 is the first launch where all of it is aimed in one direction.
