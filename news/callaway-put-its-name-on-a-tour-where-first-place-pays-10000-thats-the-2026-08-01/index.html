Callaway Put Its Name on a Tour Where First Place Pays $10,000. That's the Smart Money.

Callaway's naming rights on the ANNIKA WAPT points race are cheap pipeline spending with a long clock. Kate Villegas's first win shows why it matters.

By Travis R.  ·  August 2, 2026  ·  Clubs  ·  3 min read

Image: The Golf Wire

The last time a club company bought naming rights to a developmental tour, it was 1990 and the Ben Hogan Company was attaching itself to the PGA Tour's second tier. The name lasted three seasons before Nike took over the title. The relationships lasted considerably longer. John Daly, Tom Lehman, and a generation of grinders spent a formative year playing under a golf brand's banner at a point in their careers when almost nobody else was paying attention to them.

Callaway is running a quieter version of that play in women's golf, and it produced a headline Friday. Kate Villegas closed with a 69 to win the inaugural Wisconsin Women's Championship at TPC Wisconsin at 11 under, three clear of the field, for her first professional victory and a check for $10,000. The win moved her to third in the Callaway Race 2 Qualifying standings, the points list on the ANNIKA Women's All Pro Tour that determines who advances toward Epson Tour status. Villegas and runner-up Katherine Lemke both earned exemptions into the Epson Tour's Smoky Mountain Championship in August.

The economics here are worth stating plainly. A winner's share of $10,000 puts the total purse somewhere in the range of what a single mid-tier LPGA staff contract costs annually. Naming rights on the season-long points race are cheaper still. For a company operating under the capital discipline that came with unwinding the Topgolf merger, buying a decade of goodwill at developmental-tour prices is one of the few marketing line items that survives a budget review without an argument. This is not brand awareness spending. Nobody is buying a Paradym because they saw a points list. It is pipeline spending, which is a different thing entirely and is measured on a much longer clock.

The Annika connection is not decorative. Sorenstam played Callaway equipment through the bulk of her prime, a staff relationship that ran across the most dominant stretch any woman has had in the modern game. A tour carrying her name, sponsored by her old equipment company, sponsoring the points race that feeds the LPGA, is an annuity on a partnership signed in the nineties. Very few brands in this category have that kind of durable relationship capital available to deploy. Titleist has it with Vokey and the short-game specialists. Ping has it in junior golf. Callaway has it here, and it is finally spending it.

There is also a geographic story in this one. The Wisconsin Women's Championship is the first professional women's event held in the state since the Thornberry Creek LPGA Classic folded after 2019 when its title sponsorship went away. The WAPT is filling the map holes the LPGA schedule keeps leaving behind, and doing it in markets with real golf populations and no local professional women's golf to watch. For an equipment brand, showing up in Madison in June is more useful than another logo in a market already saturated with tour golf. Independent retailers in the upper Midwest will notice a Callaway banner at TPC Wisconsin in a way they would never notice one at a Florida swing event.

Callaway sat flat month over month at fourth in the DORMIED Index this cycle, which is roughly what a brand of its scale looks like between launch windows: no volatility, no news spike, visibility accumulated in small consistent placements rather than one loud moment. The question that determines whether this sponsorship was worth the line item is a boring one, and it will not resolve for three or four years. Do WAPT graduates show up on the LPGA with Callaway bags on their shoulders, and did the brand get there before Ping or Titleist made a call? That is the entire thesis. Watch the WITB posts from Epson Tour Q-School in two seasons. The answer will be in them.
