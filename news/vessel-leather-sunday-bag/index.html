Nobody Needs a Leather Sunday Bag. Vessel Priced One Anyway, and It Will Sell.

Vessel's Sunday IV Leather and ECCO's $300 BIOM C5 Premium launched days apart. Both sell want, not need, and both use the same price ladder logic.

By James K.  ·  September 3, 2026  ·  Bags  ·  3 min read

Image: MyGolfSpy

What Happened

Vessel released the Sunday IV Leather this week, a day before ECCO's $300 BIOM C5 Premium spikeless shoe. Both are halo products built to anchor a price ladder rather than sell volume. ECCO ranges from $130 on sale to $300 across its lineup. Vessel, ranked 56th of 215 and flat month over month, controls its own manufacturing, which makes low-volume leather runs viable.

The question that follows every premium accessory launch is whether the thing is worth the money, and it is the least useful question anyone can ask. Vessel's new Sunday IV Leather landed this week, followed a day later by ECCO's $300 BIOM C5 Premium spikeless shoe, and both launches share a customer who has already decided the answer before the press release goes out.

Start with what a Sunday bag actually is. It is, by definition, the second bag a golfer owns. Nobody's only bag is a Sunday bag. You cannot fit a full set in it, you cannot ride with it comfortably, and it exists for the nine holes after work and the range session where a cart bag feels absurd. That makes it the purest additive purchase in the equipment aisle. There is no trade-in, no upgrade path, no old bag being replaced. Wrapping that bag in full leather is not solving a problem. It is decorating a solution the buyer already has.

Which is fine, and the industry should stop being coy about it. The instructive comparison is ECCO, a company that owns its tanneries, its factories, its distribution and more than 2,000 retail doors across 88 countries. That vertical integration is what lets ECCO run a real price ladder: entry styles down around $130 on sale, mainline product in the $190 to $220 band, BIOM premium models from $250 to $300. The C5 Premium, with its GORE-TEX SURROUND construction and an aluminum BOA dial replacing the usual plastic one, is not built to be the volume seller. It is built to sit at the top of the wall and make the $210 shoe next to it look like restraint.

Vessel runs the same play with the same structural advantage. It controls its own manufacturing, which is the only reason a brand this size can justify a low-volume leather run without wrecking its margin math on the core carry bags. The Sunday IV Leather is a ceiling product. Its job is to anchor the range, generate the photography, and give the customer who was already going to spend $400 a reason to spend more. Halo items do not need to sell in numbers. They need to exist, be seen, and be believable, and leather is believable in a way another colorway is not.

The risk is that the halo stops working. Premium accessory launches have a shelf life measured in how often they arrive, and a brand that drops a top-tier leather edition twice a year is building a range while a brand that drops one every quarter is just running a promotion calendar. Vessel sits 56th of 215 globally and moved zero percent month over month, which is roughly what you would expect from a launch cadence that maintains attention rather than expanding it. Leather drops hold the audience you have. They are not the thing that recruits the golfer currently carrying a Ping Hoofer and wondering what the fuss is.

The more interesting read is on the aluminum BOA dial, because that is the kind of detail that separates a genuine premium build from a premium price. A plastic dial fails eventually, everyone who has owned one knows it, and swapping in metal costs real money for a benefit no marketing department can put in a hero shot. Vessel's leather has to clear the same bar. Hand-finished hide that scuffs badly in year two is a $600 lesson. Hide that patinas is the reason someone buys the second one.

Watch what Vessel does underneath this bag over the next two seasons. The premium editions are the loudest part of the range and the least important part of the business. If the halo is doing its job, the mainline carry bags get more expensive without anyone objecting, and the brand quietly moves up a tier in a category where the customer decides on materials and remembers on the strap.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is a leather Sunday bag considered a want rather than a need?

A Sunday bag is by definition a golfer's second bag. It cannot hold a full set and is not built for riding, so buying one is purely additive rather than a replacement or upgrade. Adding leather decorates a solution the buyer already owns.

What is a halo product and how does Vessel use one?

A halo product sits at the top of a range to anchor pricing and generate attention, not to sell in volume. The Sunday IV Leather makes the rest of Vessel's lineup look more reasonable by comparison and gives higher-spending customers a reason to trade up.

How does ECCO's price ladder work?

ECCO runs entry-level styles around $130 on sale, mainline product between $190 and $220, and premium BIOM models from $250 to $300. The $300 BIOM C5 Premium exists partly to make the $210 shoe next to it look restrained.

What makes the ECCO BIOM C5 Premium different from a standard BOA shoe?

It uses an aluminum BOA dial rather than the usual plastic one, alongside GORE-TEX SURROUND construction. The metal dial costs real money for a durability benefit that does not photograph, which is the sort of detail that separates a genuine premium build from a premium price.

Does the Sunday IV Leather move Vessel's brand position?

Not on its own. Vessel sits 56th of 215 globally and was flat month over month, consistent with a launch cadence that maintains an existing audience rather than recruiting new buyers from competitors.
