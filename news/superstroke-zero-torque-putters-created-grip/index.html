Zero-Torque Putters Created a Grip Problem. SuperStroke Built a SKU for It.

SuperStroke's Zenergy TLT Off Axis is a putter grip built for zero-torque heads, and a Cameron McCormick instruction piece is how it gets sold.

By James K.  ·  September 16, 2026  ·  Grips  ·  3 min read

Image: The Golf Wire

What Happened With SuperStroke?

SuperStroke released the Zenergy TLT Off Axis, a putter grip designed for zero-torque and lie-angle balanced heads that presets forward shaft lean without a forward press. It is being marketed through a putting-slump instruction piece from ambassador Cameron McCormick, Jordan Spieth's coach. SuperStroke also pushed into full-set grips with the REVL Player 360 4X.

SuperStroke's latest marketing push arrives dressed as instruction: a putting-slump primer from Cameron McCormick, Jordan Spieth's longtime coach and an official brand ambassador, built around the idea that every golfer needs a stroke blueprint and a way to check it. Buried in the back half is the part that actually matters to anyone tracking this category. The Zenergy TLT Off Axis is a putter grip designed specifically for zero-torque and lie-angle balanced heads.

That is a grip built in response to a putter head. Which is not how this category has historically worked.

Why Did SuperStroke Build the Zenergy TLT Off Axis for Zero-Torque Putters?

The TLT presets a forward-leaning shaft position at address, which means lower loft at impact without the player having to forward press or load the head dynamically to get there. McCormick, who says he now has the grip on his own putter, frames it as removing a variable rather than adding one. Fair enough. The interesting part is the premise: enough golfers are now holding zero-torque and lie-angle balanced putters that a grip company sees a fitting gap worth tooling for.

Two years ago that would have been a rounding error. L.A.B. was a curiosity with a cult, and the rest of the industry treated torque balancing as a niche. It is not niche anymore. Every major putter maker has an answer on the wall, and those heads change the geometry a player wants at address, which changes what the handle should be doing. Grip companies have spent decades responding to hand size, stroke arc and pressure, all of it player-side. Responding to head architecture is new territory, and it is a smarter place to be than the eleventh colorway of a pistol taper.

The structural point is about the fitting bay. Grips are a consumable with a replacement cycle, and the sale almost always happens during a fitting conversation. A new head category resets that conversation from scratch. Whoever has a credible off-the-shelf answer when a fitter says "this head wants a different handle" gets the default recommendation, and defaults in the grip business are worth more than tour counts.

How Does Instruction Content Sell Putter Grips?

The McCormick piece is a good example of a tactic more brands should steal. It spends most of its length on genuinely useful stuff: distance from the ball, eye position, why tour players litter the practice green with mirrors and alignment aids, and the argument that Xander Schauffele and J.T. Poston putt so differently that neither is a template for anyone else. Then it lands on grips as the fitting variable. Poston uses his hands more, so a smaller diameter makes sense. A player trying to quiet the hands wants more girth to lock things down.

That is a diagnostic that terminates in a SKU, and it works because it is true. Compare it to the alternative SuperStroke has leaned on for a decade, which is being the most-used putter grip in professional golf. Tour count buys awareness. It does not tell a 12-handicap why he should spend forty dollars this month instead of next year.

What Is SuperStroke's Position in the Grips Category Now?

Fifteen years in, the harder fight is the one outside the putter bag. The REVL Player 360 4X, with an enlarged lower hand profile meant to simulate four extra wraps of tape, is SuperStroke pushing into full-set grips, where Golf Pride and Lamkin have the OEM relationships and the retail pegboard. The brand sits 43rd of 215 in our index and flat month over month, which reads about right for a company whose product line is expanding faster than its brand noise.

The bet worth watching is the TLT. If zero-torque holds as a permanent putter category rather than a three-season trend, SuperStroke will have been the first grip company to build for it, and that is a genuinely rare position for an accessories brand to occupy. If it fades, they burned a mold. Either way, a grip maker reacting to head design instead of hand size is the most interesting thing this company has done in a while.

What Else Do Golfers Ask About SuperStroke?

What does the SuperStroke Zenergy TLT Off Axis actually do?

It presets a forward-leaning shaft position at address, producing lower loft at impact without the player needing a forward press or dynamic loading of the head. It is built for zero-torque and lie-angle balanced putter heads.

Why is a grip designed around a putter head significant?

Grip companies have historically built around player variables like hand size, stroke arc and grip pressure. Building for head architecture is a response to hardware, and it positions SuperStroke as the default recommendation when a fitter says a zero-torque head needs a different handle.

What is Cameron McCormick's role here?

McCormick is an official SuperStroke brand ambassador and Jordan Spieth's longtime coach. His putting-slump instruction piece covers stroke fundamentals and feedback tools before landing on grip diameter as a fitting variable. He says he now uses the TLT on his own putter.

What is the REVL Player 360 4X?

A full-set grip with an enlarged lower hand profile meant to simulate four extra wraps of tape, aimed at more even grip pressure. It represents SuperStroke pushing outside putter grips, where Golf Pride and Lamkin hold the OEM and retail advantage.

Does grip diameter depend on how a player uses their hands?

According to McCormick, yes. He cites J.T. Poston, who uses his hands more in the stroke and may prefer a smaller grip, versus players trying to limit hand movement, who often choose a larger diameter to lock things down.
