The 329,000-Round Arccos Study That Should End the 'Drive for Show, Putt for Dough' Debate

Arccos analyzed 329,000 rounds and found putting accounts for just 14-20% of the handicap gap. The 'drive for show, putt for dough' cliché doesn't survive the data.

By Travis  ·  July 20, 2026  ·  Shot Tracking  ·  3 min read

Image: MyGolfSpy

Arccos just handed MyGolfSpy the data to bury one of golf's most repeated aphorisms, and the numbers are more decisive than the resulting article lets on. Across 329,000 rounds, putting accounted for 14 to 20 percent of the stroke gap between handicap levels. Tee shots accounted for 24 to 29 percent. Approach play, the category the cliché ignores entirely, made up 38 to 40 percent.

The underlying insight is not new. Mark Broadie's strokes-gained framework, first published in 2008 and adopted by the PGA Tour in 2011, made the same argument with tour data. What is new is the sample size on the amateur side. Broadie's early work relied on ShotLink, which only captured professional play. Arccos has now built the amateur equivalent, and the 329,000-round figure is what happens when a sensor company spends a decade collecting shot data from actual weekend golfers. That data set is the real product. The subscription revenue is how they fund it.

The more interesting number from the 2026 Arccos Driving Distance Report is not the 63-yard gap between scratch and 30-handicap drivers. It is the miss severity. Twelve percent of drives from the 0 to 4.9 group ended in a penalty or forced recovery. That figure jumped to 45.1 percent for players above 30. Fairway percentage barely moved, only ten points. The difference is not accuracy in the traditional sense. It is the cost of being wrong. Better drivers miss into the rough. Worse drivers miss into trouble that requires a full stroke to escape.

That reframes what driver fitting actually optimizes for. The industry has spent the last five years marketing distance, with TaylorMade, Callaway, and Ping all leaning on ball-speed gains as the primary sales pitch. The Arccos data suggests the more valuable outcome for the majority of buyers is miss dispersion, not peak speed. This is the argument Ping has been making quietly with the G430 Max 10K and that TaylorMade's Qi35 Max is structurally built around. The data supports them. The marketing budgets, mostly, still chase the long-drive crowd.

For Arccos specifically, the study is a reminder that the company's competitive moat is not the sensors. Shot Scope and Garmin both make competent alternatives, and Garmin's distribution advantage is significant. Arccos wins by being the source golf media cites when it needs to say something true about how amateurs actually play. Every MyGolfSpy article that references Arccos data is a marketing asset the company did not have to buy. The 28th-ranked brand in DORMIED's global index punches above its weight in editorial visibility for exactly this reason.

The cliché will survive this article, of course. Golf's oral tradition is durable in a way data is not. But the operating question for equipment brands is whether they are still building product for the version of the game the cliché describes, or the version the numbers describe. Approach play is 40 percent of the improvement gap and gets a fraction of the R&D marketing spend that drivers receive. That is a category imbalance, and the next brand to solve for it, with a genuinely differentiated iron or hybrid story rather than another wedge refresh, will find an audience Arccos has already mapped.
