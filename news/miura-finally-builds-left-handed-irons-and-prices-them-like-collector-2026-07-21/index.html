Miura Finally Builds Left-Handed Irons, and Prices Them Like Collector Pieces

Miura Golf launches its first-ever left-handed forged irons, the LT-820 and LC-930, at $400 per head under its Small Batch program.

By Travis R.  ·  July 21, 2026  ·  Clubs  ·  3 min read

Image: The Golf Wire

What Happened With Miura Golf?

Miura released its first left-handed irons, the LT-820 player's cavity and LC-930 players distance, through its Small Batch program at $400 per head, or $3,200 for a 4-PW plus gap wedge set before shafts, grips, or fitting. That is roughly double a Titleist T150 set at $1,600 or a PXG 0311 ST at $2,400.

Left-handed golfers represent roughly 5 to 7% of the global playing population, and for most of Miura's 68-year history, that math has been the reason the Himeji factory has not built for them. That changes with the LT-820 and LC-930, the first left-handed forgings Miura has ever produced under its Small Batch program.

What Are the Miura LT-820 and LC-930 Irons?

The LT-820 is a player's cavity inspired by the CB-1008. The LC-930 is a players distance iron inspired by the CB-302, and Miura is calling it the longest forged cavity-back the company has ever built. Both are available 4-PW plus gap wedge at $400 per head, which puts a full eight-iron set at $3,200 before shafts, grips, or fitting. Small Batch means limited quantities and no promise of replenishment.

The pricing is worth sitting with. A comparable Titleist T150 set runs about $1,600 street. A PXG 0311 ST set with premium shafts lands around $2,400. Miura is asking left-handed buyers to pay roughly double the premium-OEM rate for irons that, by the brand's own framing, may never be produced again. That is not a mainstream play. It is a scarcity play aimed at a subset of a subset: left-handed golfers who already understand forged Japanese steel and have the discretionary budget to treat clubs as objects. Miura has been running that playbook for years on the right-handed side. The question is whether the lefty market, which has historically been starved rather than courted, responds to exclusivity or resents the surcharge.

Why Are Forged Left-Handed Irons So Hard to Find?

The left-handed forged market has been thin for a long time. Mizuno builds most of its Grain Flow Forged line in left-handed, but often on a made-to-order timeline. Srixon does the ZX line in lefty. Titleist covers the T-series. Ping offers the Blueprint T and S in lefty on request. Beyond that, the availability curve drops off fast, and the boutique forged category, Epon, Kyoei, Fujimoto, has been almost entirely right-handed. Phil Mickelson spent much of his career gaming Callaway blades built as one-offs because the mainstream forged options for elite lefties barely existed. Miura entering that space, even in a limited run, is a real structural addition to the category. It just arrives with a price tag that assumes the buyer has been waiting a long time and is willing to pay for the wait.

The 8AM Golf ownership context matters here. Since Howard Milstein consolidated Miura, True Spec, Fairway Jockey, GOLF.com, and more recently McLaren Golf and PAYNTR under one roof, Miura has moved steadily from pure craft brand toward a more programmatic release cadence. Small Batch drops, category-specific launches, tighter coordination with True Spec fitting bays. The LT-820 and LC-930 fit that pattern. They are not a response to demand Miura discovered last quarter. They are a scheduled expansion into a demographic the parent company can now market to through its own media and fitting channels. GOLF.com covering a Miura left-handed launch is not a coincidence of timing.

What Sets Miura Small Batch Forgings Apart?

The forgings themselves look serious. Y-Grind sole on the LT-820, cambered sole on the LC-930, no speed slots or tungsten screws or urethane injections on either. That last detail is the tell. Miura is drawing a line between forged feel and the engineered-distance category most OEMs are chasing, and the LC-930 is positioned as proof that forgiveness can be built through geometry alone. Whether that argument holds against a face-cup iron in a launch monitor bay is a separate question. Miura's customer base has never particularly cared about that bay.

Miura sits at #23 in the global brand index this month, flat month-over-month, which is roughly where a low-volume, high-margin craft brand should sit. The left-handed launch will not move that number materially. What it signals is more interesting: Miura, under 8AM, is now willing to build for markets it previously wrote off as uneconomic. The next test is whether Small Batch stays genuinely small, or whether the designation quietly softens as the release calendar fills out.
