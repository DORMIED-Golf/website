Thirty-Plus Parameters, Three That Matter: TrackMan's Data Advantage Cuts Both Ways

MyGolfSpy says ignore five TrackMan launch monitor numbers. The advice exposes a usability problem inside TrackMan's biggest competitive advantage.

By Travis R.  ·  August 17, 2026  ·  Launch Monitors  ·  3 min read

Image: MyGolfSpy

What Happened

MyGolfSpy advised golfers to ignore five TrackMan outputs during practice: spin loft, swing plane, swing direction, dynamic lie and spin axis. The advice targets usability, not accuracy, and lands on the brand whose moat is measurement completeness across 30-plus parameters. TrackMan already built the fix in 2011 with the Combine, a single score out of 100. It ranks 27th on the DORMIED Index, down 18.3 percent month over month.

Doppler radar does not decide what to report. It resolves what it can resolve and hands the whole ledger over, which is precisely why TrackMan built a category-defining business: for years nobody else could resolve as much. Ball speed, club speed, attack angle, club path, face-to-path, dynamic loft, spin loft, swing direction, spin axis, dynamic lie. North of 30 measured and calculated outputs, all arriving in the two seconds after impact.

MyGolfSpy just published a piece telling golfers to ignore five of them. Spin loft, swing plane, swing direction, dynamic lie and spin axis go on the do-not-watch list, not because they are wrong or useless, but because a golfer working on a swing change while monitoring eight variables is doing something closer to data entry than practice. The recommendation is to pick a goal, pick two or three numbers that measure it, and let the rest sit in the background.

That is a usability critique dressed as an instruction article, and it lands on the brand whose competitive moat is measurement completeness. TrackMan's parameter count was the whole pitch when the customer was a tour player, a national team coach or a fitter with a $25,000 line item. Those users have the vocabulary to know that swing direction and club path are different things, and that only one of them describes what happens at impact. The home simulator buyer does not, and the number of home simulator buyers now dwarfs the professional install base.

Worth noting that TrackMan solved this problem for itself fifteen years ago and then largely stopped talking about it. The Combine, introduced in 2011, compresses a structured 60-shot test into a single score out of 100 against handicap-normalized benchmarks. One number, comparable across sessions, no interpretation required. It remains one of the more elegant pieces of consumer product design in golf technology, and it exists inside a software environment that still defaults to showing everything at once. Arccos and Shot Scope built entire businesses on the opposite instinct: fewer outputs, ranked by what actually changes scores, and a willingness to hide the raw data from users who cannot act on it.

The competitive pressure here is not about accuracy. Garmin's R10 sits around $600 and Rapsodo's MLM2Pro is in the same neighborhood, and neither is trying to out-measure a five-figure radar unit. They are trying to out-simplify it, which is a fight over the category's vocabulary rather than its physics. Whoever the majority of golfers use is who teaches the majority of golfers what to look at. TrackMan currently gets the best of that arrangement and the worst of it, because the brand has been genericized. Golfers say "TrackMan numbers" the way they say "Vokey grind," which is a marketing outcome most companies would trade a tour staff for, and also a reason the brand rarely gets discussed as a brand.

The DORMIED Index puts TrackMan 27th of 215 this month, down 18.3 percent from May, which is roughly what happens to a hardware company with no product cycle in the middle of the northern hemisphere golf season. The conversation moves to what people are playing, not what is measuring them. Genericized brands tend to run flat between launches because the name is already in circulation as a unit of measurement rather than a purchase decision.

The interesting question is whether TrackMan treats data filtering as a software feature or leaves it to third parties. Skill-level presets, a default dashboard that shows three parameters instead of twelve, a practice mode that hides everything not relevant to the stated goal: none of that is hard engineering for a company that already built the Combine. It is a product philosophy decision. Right now the filtering is being done by golf media on TrackMan's behalf, for free, and not entirely on TrackMan's terms.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which TrackMan numbers does MyGolfSpy say golfers should ignore?

Spin loft, swing plane, swing direction, dynamic lie and spin axis. The argument is not that they are inaccurate or meaningless, but that most golfers cannot act on them during a practice session and are better served picking two or three numbers tied to a specific goal.

What is the difference between swing direction and club path?

Swing direction is the direction the clubhead travels at the low point of the swing arc. Club path is the direction the club is moving at impact. They can differ, which is why club path is the more useful reference for most golfers working on a ball flight problem.

What is the TrackMan Combine?

A structured 60-shot test introduced in 2011 that compresses a full session into a single score out of 100, benchmarked against handicap-normalized data. It is TrackMan's own answer to data overload, and it predates the current home simulator boom by more than a decade.

Why is TrackMan's parameter count described as a liability?

Measurement completeness sold well to tour players, national team coaches and fitters who have the vocabulary to interpret it. The home simulator buyer, now the larger market, generally does not, which makes competitors like Garmin's R10 and Rapsodo's MLM2Pro able to compete on simplicity rather than accuracy.

Why did TrackMan's DORMIED Index score fall 18.3 percent in June 2026?

No product cycle during peak northern hemisphere golf season, plus the structural effect of genericization. Golfers use "TrackMan numbers" as a unit of measurement rather than a brand consideration, which keeps conversation flat between launches.
